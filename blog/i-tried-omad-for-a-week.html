I Tried OMAD for a Week

Alex | March 23, 2026 | 5 min read

OMAD stands for One Meal a Day. It's exactly what it sounds like: you fast for twenty-three hours, then eat all your daily calories in a single hour. I first heard about it from a YouTube video with a thumbnail of a guy holding a giant plate of food and grinning. "Lose weight while eating whatever you want!" the title promised. I was skeptical but intrigued.

I decided to try it for seven days. Not because I thought it was sustainable, but because I wanted to understand the hunger patterns. What does it actually feel like to not eat for twenty-three hours? Would I be ravenous? Would I binge? Would I feel amazing and clear-headed like the YouTubers claimed?

Day one was surprisingly easy. I had black coffee in the morning, water throughout the day, and a massive dinner at 7 PM. Steak, potatoes, vegetables, a roll, and even ice cream. I hit my calorie target and felt satisfied. I went to bed thinking, "This isn't so bad."

The social isolation was worse than the hunger. Food is how we connect with people. Lunch with coworkers. Dinner with family. Happy hour with friends. When you're fasting for 23 hours, you opt out of all of it. I found myself making excuses to avoid meals with others because explaining OMAD was exhausting and watching people eat while I sipped water was depressing.

I also noticed my relationship with food became weirdly obsessive. During the one hour I was allowed to eat, I couldn't stop thinking about what I'd have. I'd plan my meal for hours in advance. I'd watch food videos on YouTube. I'd browse restaurant menus I had no intention of ordering from. The restriction made food occupy way more mental space than it should.

The physical adaptation was real but incomplete. By day four, my body had mostly adjusted to the fasting window. The morning hunger was mild. The afternoon energy was stable. But the evening binge-eating tendency never went away. No matter how disciplined I tried to be, eating 2,000 calories in one sitting always felt excessive. My stomach stretched uncomfortably. I'd feel bloated for hours.

Looking back, OMAD taught me something useful: I can handle hunger. It's not an emergency. It passes. That mental toughness has helped me in other contexts — skipping the office donuts, waiting for dinner instead of snacking, saying no to late-night cravings. But as a long-term eating pattern? Hard pass. Life is too short to spend 23 hours a day waiting for a meal.

Day two, the hunger hit at 11 AM. Not gentle hunger. Aggressive, distracting hunger. My stomach growled in a meeting. I drank more water. The hunger passed after twenty minutes, then returned at 2 PM. It came in waves. I learned that hunger is not a constant state — it spikes and fades. If you can ride out the spike, you get a few hours of peace.

Day three, I felt weirdly focused. No post-lunch energy crash. No brain fog. My code was flowing. I was productive. But by 6 PM, I was so hungry that I ate too fast and felt sick afterward. That's the danger of OMAD: when you finally allow yourself to eat, your body screams "GO" and you override your satiety signals.

By day five, my workout performance crashed. I tried to lift weights and felt weak. My body had no fuel. I cut the session short. Day six, I was irritable and short-tempered. My girlfriend asked if I was okay. I snapped at her over something trivial. Not my finest moment.

I ended the experiment on day seven. I lost two pounds, mostly water. The hunger waves were manageable but annoying. The focus was nice but not worth the mood swings. And eating one massive meal left me feeling stuffed and uncomfortable every night.

OMAD works for some people. It creates a calorie deficit by restricting eating time, not food types. But for me, it was too extreme. I prefer a moderate approach: three meals, no snacks, consistent timing. Less drama. More sustainable.

The digestive impact was another surprise. Eating 2,000+ calories in one sitting is hard on your stomach. I felt bloated and uncomfortable for hours after each meal. My digestion slowed down. I had heartburn a few times, which I never normally get. The body isn't designed to process that much food at once. It's designed for smaller, more frequent meals.

I also missed the ritual of multiple meals. Breakfast is my coffee and contemplation time. Lunch is my break from work. Dinner is social. OMAD eliminated two of those three rituals. I didn't realize how much I valued them until they were gone. Food isn't just fuel. It's structure. It's pleasure. It's connection. Taking that away left a hole that fasting couldn't fill.

What's the most extreme eating pattern you've ever tried?

If you're curious about fasting, start with something gentler. Try a 12-hour overnight fast. Then 14 hours. Then 16. See how your body responds. Some people thrive on longer fasts. Others feel terrible. There's no universal right answer. There's only your answer, which you find through experimentation, not ideology.
