I Tried Keto for One Week and Quit

Alex | March 03, 2026 | 5 min read

My coworker Dave lost twenty pounds on keto in two months. He wouldn't shut up about it. "Bro, you can eat bacon and lose weight." Every day at lunch, he'd microwave some concoction of ground beef and cheese while I ate my sad turkey sandwich. I was skeptical but also jealous. So I tried it. For one week. Then I quit.

Day one was actually fine. Eggs and bacon for breakfast. Salad with chicken and avocado for lunch. Steak and broccoli for dinner. I felt like I was eating at a steakhouse three times a day. The food was good. I wasn't hungry. I thought, maybe Dave was onto something.

Day two, the headache started. Not a normal headache. A deep, throbbing, behind-the-eyes headache that made staring at my code monitor feel like torture. I drank water. I added salt. I ate more fat. Nothing helped. By day three, I was irritable, foggy, and genuinely concerned that I might yell at someone in a meeting for no reason.

Looking back, the biggest red flag was how quickly I became food-obsessed. By day three, I was watching YouTube videos of people eating pasta. Not because I wanted pasta — because I couldn't have it. The restriction created a psychological scarcity that made forbidden foods seem more appealing than they actually were. It's the classic dieting trap: the more you can't have something, the more you want it.

I also underestimated the social cost. My friends invited me to a pizza night on day five. I went, sat there with a plate of chicken wings while everyone else enjoyed slices, and felt like an outsider. Food is social currency. When you opt out of the shared meal, you opt out of part of the bonding experience. It's subtle but real. By day six, I was avoiding social situations because I didn't want to explain my diet.

The physical symptoms were worse than I expected. Beyond the headache and fatigue, I had bad breath — "keto breath" from acetone production. My girlfriend noticed before I did. I also had digestive issues. Without fiber from grains and fruits, things slowed down considerably. Let's just say the bathroom situation was not pleasant.

The final straw was looking at my data. I tracked everything during that week. Yes, I lost three pounds. But my workout performance dropped by 30%. My sleep quality declined. My mood was terrible. And the weight came back within four days of eating normally. Net result: one week of misery for zero lasting change. That's not a diet. That's a detour.

Day four, I stepped on the scale. Down three pounds. Water weight, obviously, but still encouraging. I pushed through. Day five, I tried to go for a run and felt like I was moving through molasses. My legs had no power. I cut the run short after ten minutes and walked home feeling defeated.

Day six, I broke. I was at a friend's birthday dinner, staring at a plate of pasta while everyone else enjoyed themselves. I realized I wasn't just on a diet — I was opting out of normal social life. And for what? To lose water weight and feel terrible? I ate the pasta. It was the best pasta I've ever had.

I'm not saying keto doesn't work. Dave's twenty pounds are real. But keto works because it creates a calorie deficit by removing an entire macronutrient category, not because of some metabolic magic. And for me, the cost was too high. The brain fog, the social isolation, the athletic performance drop — none of it was worth three pounds of water.

What I learned: any diet that makes you miserable is a bad diet, regardless of what the scale says. Sustainability beats intensity every time. I'd rather lose weight slowly while eating pasta with friends than rapidly while hiding in my apartment eating cheese cubes.

The cost-benefit analysis was brutal. One week of keto cost me: one friendship dinner avoided, one gym session cut short, one argument with my girlfriend, three days of headaches, and approximately $40 in specialty keto foods. The return? Three pounds of water weight that returned in four days. That's not a diet. That's a bad trade.

I don't regret trying it, though. Experiments teach you things you can't learn from reading. I now know that I can handle low-carb for a day or two without issues, but extended ketosis makes me feel terrible. That knowledge is valuable. It stops me from being tempted by the next keto trend that comes along. I've been there. I've got the data. It doesn't work for me.

What's the shortest you've ever lasted on a diet before quitting?

The experience taught me something valuable about myself: I need dietary flexibility. Rigidity makes me rebellious. Tell me I can never eat pasta again, and suddenly pasta is all I think about. Give me the freedom to eat pasta occasionally, and I often choose not to. Autonomy matters more than any specific food rule. Keto took away my autonomy, and my brain responded by obsessing over the forbidden foods.

Would I recommend trying keto? Honestly, sure — but only as an experiment, not a lifestyle. Try it for two weeks. Track everything. Notice how you feel. Then decide if it's worth continuing. For some people, it genuinely works. For me, it was a disaster. But I wouldn't have known without testing it. Data beats dogma every time.
