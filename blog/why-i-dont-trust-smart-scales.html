Why I Don't Trust Smart Scales

Alex | July 09, 2026 | 5 min read

I own a smart scale. It cost me eighty dollars and promised to track not just weight, but body fat percentage, muscle mass, bone density, water percentage, and probably my astrological sign. It syncs with an app. It generates graphs. It sends me "insights." And I don't trust a single number it produces beyond weight.

The problem is bioelectrical impedance analysis, or BIA. That's the technology most smart scales use to estimate body composition. You step on metal electrodes, a tiny electrical current passes through your body, and the scale measures resistance. The theory is that muscle conducts electricity well (lots of water), while fat resists it. From this resistance, the scale estimates your body composition.

In practice, BIA is wildly sensitive to hydration. Drink a glass of water before weighing? Your body fat percentage drops. Dehydrated from a run? It spikes. I tested this deliberately. One morning, I weighed myself: 18.2% body fat. I drank 16 ounces of water, waited ten minutes, and weighed again: 16.8% body fat. I didn't lose body fat in ten minutes. I just added water, which conducts electricity better, making the scale think I had more muscle.

I also tested the scale's consistency over time. I weighed myself, stepped off, and immediately weighed myself again. The body fat percentage varied by 0.5% between back-to-back measurements. That's not a small margin when you're trying to track trends. A "real" change of 1% over a month could easily be measurement noise. You'd never know.

The app that comes with the scale is even worse. It generates "insights" like "Your muscle mass increased 2% this week!" based on data that I know is noisy. It creates false confidence. People see the graph trending up and think they're making progress, when really the scale is just having a good day. The app doesn't show error bars. It doesn't mention confidence intervals. It presents uncertain data as fact.

I compared my smart scale to a professional DEXA scan just to see how far off it was. The smart scale said 18.2% body fat. The DEXA scan said 21.4%. That's a 3.2% absolute difference — enormous in body composition terms. The scale was consistently low, which is actually worse than being randomly wrong because it gives you false optimism.

My current approach is to use the smart scale for weight only, measured weekly under consistent conditions. For body composition, I rely on waist circumference, progress photos, and how my clothes fit. Every six months, I consider getting a DEXA scan if I'm genuinely curious. But day-to-day? The mirror and the tape measure tell me everything I need to know.

The muscle mass reading is even worse. According to my scale, I gained four pounds of muscle in two weeks during a period when I wasn't even lifting. What actually happened? I started eating more carbs, which increased my muscle glycogen and water stores. The scale interpreted extra water in muscle tissue as extra muscle tissue. Wrong.

I still use the scale every Saturday. But I only record the weight. The body fat percentage gets a glance and a shrug. The muscle mass number might as well be random. The water percentage is literally just telling me how hydrated I am, which I already know from the color of my urine.

If you want accurate body composition data, you need DEXA scans, hydrostatic weighing, or calipers administered by someone who knows what they're doing. A $80 scale from Amazon cannot replace lab equipment, no matter how many graphs its app generates. The data looks precise — two decimal places! — but the accuracy is terrible.

My advice? Use a smart scale if you want automatic weight logging. The convenience is real. But ignore everything else. Track your weight trend, measure your waist with a tape measure, and look in the mirror. Those three things together will tell you more than any impedance-based body fat percentage ever will.

The marketing around these devices is also predatory. They sell you on the idea of "complete body analysis" for $80, when the technology inside costs maybe $5. The real product isn't the scale — it's the app subscription they try to upsell you on. "Unlock advanced insights for $9.99/month!" The insights are garbage. The subscription is a waste. But the business model works because people want to believe that technology can solve their health problems.

I've come to believe that the best health tracking is low-tech. A bathroom scale. A tape measure. A notebook. These tools don't sync to the cloud. They don't generate graphs. But they're honest. They don't pretend to know things they can't know. And they don't cost $80 plus a monthly subscription.

Do you trust your smart scale's body fat reading, or do you ignore it too?

I also compared my scale to a professional body composition analysis at a local university lab. The smart scale said 18.2% body fat. The lab equipment said 21.7%. That is not a small difference. That is the difference between lean and average. Between keep doing what you are doing and maybe adjust your approach. If I had trusted the smart scale, I would have thought I was leaner than I actually was, which might have led to complacency.

Save your money. Buy a basic digital scale for $25. Spend the rest on a good kitchen scale for portion control, or a gym membership, or a decent pair of running shoes. Those investments will improve your health. A smart scale with body composition analysis will just give you pretty graphs based on bad data.
