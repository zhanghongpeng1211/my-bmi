Carbs Are Not the Enemy

Alex | February 02, 2026 | 4 min read

The anti-carb movement has gone too far. I can't open social media without seeing someone demonizing bread, pasta, or fruit. "Carbs make you fat." "Carbs spike insulin." "Carbs are poison." It's exhausting. And mostly wrong.

Carbohydrates are your body's preferred energy source. Your brain runs on glucose. Your muscles store glycogen for high-intensity effort. Without carbs, you feel foggy, weak, and irritable. I've been there. I tried low-carb for a month. My workouts suffered. My mood tanked. And I didn't lose any more fat than I did with moderate carbs at the same calorie level.

The real issue isn't carbs. It's excess calories. And it's the type of carbs. A sweet potato and a donut are both carbs. One is nutrient-dense, fiber-rich, and slowly digested. The other is refined sugar that spikes blood glucose and leaves you hungry an hour later. Lumping them together is like saying "fat is bad" without distinguishing between salmon and deep-fried butter.

The fiber argument is important too. Whole-food carbs — fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes — are the primary source of dietary fiber in most diets. Fiber feeds gut bacteria, improves digestion, reduces cholesterol, and increases satiety. When you cut carbs indiscriminately, you often cut fiber too. I tried low-carb and spent a week constipated and miserable. Not worth it.

Athletic performance is another area where carbs shine. High-intensity exercise relies on glycogen, which comes from carbohydrates. When I cut carbs, my runs felt like wading through mud. My lifts were weaker. My recovery was slower. The "fat-adapted" athletes you see in endurance sports spent months adapting and still use carbs strategically during events. It's not a free pass to skip them.

The cultural aspect bothers me too. Demonizing carbs often means demonizing foods that people have eaten for centuries. Rice in Asia. Pasta in Italy. Bread in France. These populations aren't obese because of carbs. They're healthy because of portion control, active lifestyles, and whole-food diets. The problem isn't the carb. It's the context.

My current carb approach: prioritize whole-food sources, time them around workouts when possible, and don't fear them. Oatmeal for breakfast. Rice or potatoes with lunch. Fruit as snacks. Pasta for dinner sometimes. My weight is stable. My energy is good. My workouts are strong. Carbs aren't the enemy. They're fuel.

I eat carbs every day. Oatmeal for breakfast. Rice or potatoes with lunch. Fruit as snacks. My weight has been stable for six months. Why? Because I control portions and prioritize whole-food sources. I don't avoid carbs. I manage them.

The insulin argument is also overblown. Yes, carbs raise insulin. Yes, insulin inhibits fat burning temporarily. But over a twenty-four-hour period, what matters is total energy balance. If you eat fewer calories than you burn, you lose fat — regardless of insulin levels. The human body is more complex than single-hormone theories suggest.

I'm not saying everyone should eat high-carb. Individual responses vary. Some people feel better on lower carbs. Some feel better on higher carbs. The key is finding what works for your body, your activity level, and your preferences — not blindly following a trend that treats an entire macronutrient category as evil.

Eat carbs. Just eat the good ones, in reasonable amounts, as part of a balanced diet. It's not complicated. We've been doing it for thousands of years.

The gut microbiome argument is worth mentioning too. Fiber-rich carbs feed beneficial gut bacteria, which produce short-chain fatty acids that reduce inflammation, improve insulin sensitivity, and support immune function. When you cut carbs indiscriminately, you starve these bacteria. The long-term consequences — poor digestion, weakened immunity, increased inflammation — are more serious than any temporary weight loss benefit.

I also think the anti-carb movement has created a weird moral hierarchy around food. Protein is "good." Fat is "acceptable." Carbs are "bad." This is nonsense. Food doesn't have morality. A sweet potato isn't virtuous. A donut isn't sinful. They're just different foods with different nutritional profiles. The context — how much, how often, what else you're eating — matters far more than any single macronutrient category.

Eat food. Real food. Mostly plants. Some protein. Some carbs. Some fat. Not too much. Michael Pollan said it better than I ever could, and he was right. The obsession with macronutrient ratios misses the bigger picture. The quality of your food matters more than the ratio. A diet of whole foods in reasonable portions will beat any macro manipulation.
