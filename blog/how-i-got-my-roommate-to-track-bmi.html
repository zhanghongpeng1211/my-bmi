How I Got My Roommate to Track BMI

Alex | February 18, 2026 | 5 min read

My roommate Jake is the opposite of me. He doesn't track anything. He doesn't weigh himself. He eats what he wants, when he wants, and somehow maintains a normal weight through sheer luck and a fast metabolism. I've been trying to get him to care about health data for two years. He finally started tracking last month. Here's how it happened.

The mistake I made initially was lecturing. I'd send him articles about BMI. I'd explain why tracking matters. I'd show him my charts. He'd nod politely and change the subject. Nobody likes being told what to do, especially by their roommate. I was coming across as preachy, and he was tuning me out.

The turning point was his annual physical. His doctor mentioned that his blood pressure was creeping up and his waist circumference had increased by two inches in a year. Not alarming, but trending wrong. Jake came home annoyed. "I don't feel unhealthy," he said. "How can I be getting worse without noticing?"

The resistance I initially faced from Jake wasn't unique. Most people don't want to be told what to do with their health. They especially don't want to hear it from someone they live with. I had to learn to shut up. To stop sending articles. To stop mentioning my own progress. To let him come to it on his own terms. That was the hardest part — resisting the urge to help.

What actually worked was making it look effortless. I'd weigh myself on Saturday mornings while making coffee. Two minutes, done. Jake would see me do it and eventually asked, "That's it? Just step on and write it down?" I showed him the chart. He was genuinely surprised by how little effort it took. The barrier to entry was lower than he thought.

I also emphasized the privacy aspect. "No app. No account. No cloud. Your data stays on your computer." That mattered to him. He's paranoid about data privacy, like a lot of people in tech. Knowing his weight history wasn't being uploaded to some company's server made the whole thing feel safer.

Now we have a friendly competition. Not explicit — just both of us tracking, both of us occasionally mentioning numbers. "Down half a pound this week." "Nice, I'm flat." It's low-pressure accountability. We don't judge each other. We don't give advice unless asked. We just share data. And somehow, that simple act of shared tracking has made both of us more consistent than we were alone.

That was my opening. I didn't lecture. I just said, "Want to see something weird?" and showed him my weight chart from the past year. He was fascinated by the patterns. The weekend spikes. The post-vacation jumps. The slow, steady downward trend. "You can see all that from weighing yourself once a week?" he asked.

I set him up with the BMI calculator on this site. No account needed. No app to download. Just a web page that stores data locally. I showed him how to export to CSV. I showed him the chart. It took five minutes. He weighed himself that Saturday and entered his first data point.

Four weeks later, he's still tracking. He texts me his Saturday number every week. He's not obsessed with it — he just likes seeing the line move. Last week he noticed his own weekend rebound pattern. "I'm up two pounds every Monday," he said. "Just like you." He felt seen. The data validated his experience.

The lesson: you can't convince someone to care about their health. They have to want it. But once they show even a flicker of interest, give them the simplest possible tool and let them discover the value themselves. Don't overwhelm them with information. Don't push. Just make it easy and get out of the way.

Jake still eats pizza. He still drinks beer. But now he knows his numbers. And knowing is the first step toward changing — if and when he decides he wants to.

The lesson generalizes beyond health tracking. You can't convince anyone of anything by arguing. People change when they're ready, not when you want them to. The best thing you can do is live your own life well, make your own habits visible, and let people come to you when they're curious. It's slower. It's less satisfying. But it's the only approach that actually works.

Jake and I now have a Saturday morning ritual. Coffee, weigh-in, chart check. It takes ten minutes. Sometimes we talk about our numbers. Sometimes we don't. The ritual itself is what matters — a weekly checkpoint that keeps us both honest. I never would have predicted that my annoying health obsession would become a bonding activity. But here we are.

The best thing you can do is live your own life well, make your own habits visible, and let people come to you when they are curious. It is slower. It is less satisfying. But it is the only approach that actually works. Jake did not start tracking because I convinced him. He started because he saw it working for me, and he wanted that for himself.

Health is personal. Change is slow. Influence is gentle. If you want to help someone, start by helping yourself. Be the example. Be consistent. Be visible. And when they're ready, they'll ask. That's when you share what you've learned. Not before. Never before.
